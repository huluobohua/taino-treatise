Chapter Six

The Colonial Encounter

On October 12, 1492, Columbus's three ships anchored off an island the Lucayan Taíno called Guanahaní. Columbus renamed it San Salvador. The people who met him on the beach were the outermost extension of an Arawakan civilization that had taken seventeen centuries to move from the Orinoco delta to the Bahamas, and they would be destroyed within a single generation. Columbus noted in his journal that they were generous, physically strong, and would make fine servants. He seized several of them and continued south.1

First Landfall and the Founding of La Navidad

Columbus sailed from Guanahaní through the Bahamas, reaching the northern coast of Hispaniola in December 1492. There the expedition entered the territory of the cacicazgo of Marién, governed by the cacique Guacanagaríx. On Christmas Eve, the Santa María ran aground on a reef off the coast. The ship was beyond saving. Guacanagaríx sent his people to help unload the cargo, and Columbus, with the wreckage and salvaged timber, constructed a small fort he named La Navidad. He left thirty-nine men behind when he departed for Spain in January 1493, instructing them to trade for gold and await his return.2

They did not survive. When Columbus returned in November 1493 with seventeen ships and over a thousand colonists, he found La Navidad burned to the ground. The garrison was dead. Accounts varied. Guacanagaríx claimed that the neighboring cacique Caonabó had attacked the settlement; other informants described Spanish misconduct (seizure of women, demands for gold) that provoked the response. The truth is irrecoverable, but the pattern established at La Navidad repeated itself across the Caribbean for the next fifty years: initial hospitality, escalating Spanish demands, indigenous resistance, and Spanish retaliation disproportionate to anything the original offense could have warranted.3

Columbus abandoned the site and founded a new settlement, La Isabela, on the northern coast in January 1494. It was the first planned European town in the Americas. It was also a catastrophe. The colonists were mostly soldiers and minor nobility expecting quick wealth. They found a humid, disease-prone coast, limited gold deposits, and an indigenous population whose cooperation they could compel only by force. La Isabela lasted barely four years before being abandoned in favor of Santo Domingo on the southern coast. During those years, Columbus imposed a tribute system on the Taíno of the interior: every person over the age of fourteen in gold-bearing provinces was required to deliver a hawk's bell full of gold dust every three months. Those who failed had their hands cut off.4

The Encomienda System

The repartimiento de indios, formalized in 1499 and expanded under the encomienda system, replaced Columbus's ad hoc tribute demands with an institutional structure. Under the encomienda, individual Spanish colonists (encomenderos) were granted authority over a specified number of Taíno. The encomendero was theoretically obligated to provide Christian instruction and protection; in return, he could extract labor and tribute from the people assigned to him. In practice, the obligation to instruct was ignored, and the right to extract was unlimited.5

Nicolás de Ovando, who served as governor of Hispaniola from 1502 to 1509, systematized the encomienda into the island's primary economic institution. Under Ovando, Taíno were forced into gold mining operations in the Cibao and San Cristóbal regions, working in shifts that kept them away from their own conucos (agricultural plots) for months at a time. They were also conscripted for construction, road building, and agricultural labor on Spanish plantations. The mining was particularly destructive. It required panning or digging in river sediments, standing in water for hours, and carrying heavy loads of earth. Oviedo, who witnessed the operations, described workers collapsing in the riverbeds.6

The encomienda did more than extract labor. It disarticulated the social structures on which Taíno life depended. Caciques lost authority over their own people, who were reassigned to Spanish masters. Communal agriculture, organized around the cacique's household and the labor obligations of the nitaínos, could not function when workers were rotated through distant mining camps. Ceremonial life, centered on the batey and the cohoba ritual, was suppressed as idolatrous. The system did not merely exploit the Taíno; it dismantled the political, economic, and spiritual framework within which Taíno identity had meaning.7

The Jaragua Massacre

In 1503, Ovando carried out the single most notorious act of the conquest of Hispaniola. The cacicazgo of Jaragua, in the southwestern part of the island, was the largest and most culturally accomplished of the five paramount chiefdoms. Its ruler was the cacica Anacaona, widow of Caonabó and sister of the previous cacique Bohechío. She was a poet and composer of areítos (ceremonial song-narratives), and her court attracted nitaínos from across Hispaniola.8

Ovando traveled to Jaragua in the autumn of 1503, ostensibly to collect tribute and inspect the province. He invited Anacaona and her assembled nitaínos to a feast. When they gathered in the large caney (chief's house), Ovando gave a prearranged signal. Spanish soldiers surrounded the building and set it on fire. Those who escaped the flames were killed with swords. Anacaona herself was not burned; she was seized, taken to Santo Domingo, given a show trial, and hanged in the public square. Las Casas, who arrived on Hispaniola the year before, recorded the event. He wrote that Ovando had eighty nitaínos burned alive in the caney and that Anacaona's execution was carried out to prevent the reorganization of Taíno resistance in the west.9

Demographic Collapse

The pre-contact population of Hispaniola is one of the most contested numbers in colonial history. The debate matters because it determines the scale of what followed. A low initial population implies a smaller catastrophe; a high one makes the Spanish colonial system one of the most destructive episodes in recorded human history.

The "high counters," following the methods of Sherburne Cook and Woodrow Borah (who estimated pre-contact central Mexico's population at 25 million), have proposed figures for Hispaniola ranging from several hundred thousand to over a million. The most frequently cited high estimate is around one million, based on extrapolations from early colonial tribute records and settlement density reports.10 Conservative scholars, including Angel Rosenblat, have placed the figure closer to 100,000, arguing that the island's carrying capacity and the archaeological evidence of settlement density do not support higher numbers.11 Samuel Wilson, synthesizing the evidence in the 1990s, suggested a range of 250,000 to 750,000, acknowledging that precision is impossible given the available data.12

Whatever the starting number, the trajectory is not in dispute. The first reliable colonial census, conducted in 1508 under Ovando's administration, counted roughly 60,000 indigenous people on Hispaniola. A more detailed repartimiento count in 1514 found approximately 32,000. By 1519, a smallpox epidemic killed much of the surviving population. By the mid-sixteenth century, the indigenous community had been reduced to a few hundred individuals, almost all of them living in marginal areas or attached to Spanish households. By 1565, the count stood at perhaps 200. In 1802, Spanish colonial authorities formally declared the Taíno of Hispaniola extinct.13

The causes were multiple and interacting. Epidemic diseases, particularly smallpox and measles, struck populations with no acquired immunity. The effects were catastrophic, but disease alone does not explain the speed or completeness of the collapse. The encomienda's forced labor removed people from subsistence agriculture, causing widespread malnutrition. Social disruption, the separation of families, the destruction of cacicazgo authority, the suppression of religious practice, produced what some scholars have described as a collective loss of will to survive. Birth rates fell. Suicide, documented by multiple Spanish observers, increased. Las Casas reported that mothers killed their infants rather than see them grow up under Spanish rule.14

Lynne Guitar and others have argued that the encomienda's dismantling of Taíno social organization preceded and enabled the worst disease impacts. A population with intact political structures, functioning agriculture, and social cohesion could have weathered epidemics as other indigenous populations did, with devastating losses but not annihilation. The Taíno of Hispaniola faced epidemics while simultaneously starving, overworked, and stripped of the communal systems that could have organized care for the sick and maintained food production during crises.15

Las Casas's Testimony

Bartolomé de las Casas arrived on Hispaniola in 1502, at the age of eighteen, and received an encomienda of his own. For the next twelve years he participated in the system he would later spend his life denouncing. His conversion came in 1514, during a sermon he was preparing, when he confronted the passage in Ecclesiasticus: "The bread of the needy is their life: he that defraudeth him thereof is a man of blood." He renounced his encomienda, joined the Dominican order, and began documenting what he had witnessed and participated in.16

Two of his works bear directly on the Taíno. The Brevísima relación de la destrucción de las Indias, completed in 1542 and published in 1552, is a compressed catalogue of Spanish atrocities across the Americas. Its descriptions of Hispaniola are specific: infants dashed against rocks, people hunted with dogs, mass hangings in groups of thirteen (in honor, the Spaniards said, of Christ and the twelve apostles). The Apologética Historia Sumaria, left in manuscript and not published until the nineteenth century, is a more systematic ethnographic and philosophical work. It describes Taíno society, religion, agriculture, and governance in detail, arguing that the Taíno met every classical criterion for rational, civilized life and that their subjugation was therefore unjust by the Spaniards' own legal and theological standards.17

Las Casas's population estimates have been the most debated aspect of his testimony. He claimed that Hispaniola held three million inhabitants before the conquest, a figure most modern scholars consider too high. His critics, beginning with the chronicler Oviedo in the sixteenth century, accused him of exaggeration for polemical purposes: the higher the pre-contact population, the greater the crime. This is likely true; Las Casas was writing to persuade, and his numbers served his argument. But dismissing his population figures does not discredit his descriptions of the system's operation. His accounts of the encomienda, the mining operations, and specific events like the Jaragua massacre are corroborated by Oviedo, by royal officials' correspondence, and by the demographic record itself. The numbers may be inflated; the mechanism he described was real.18

Las Casas also played a direct role in the events he documented. He mentored the young Guarocuya, baptized as Enriquillo, during the latter's education in a Dominican monastery. Decades later, he served as an intermediary in the negotiations that ended Enriquillo's rebellion. His influence on Spanish colonial law, including the New Laws of 1542 that formally (if ineffectively) abolished the encomienda, was substantial. He was not only a witness but an actor in the history he recorded, a fact that complicates but does not invalidate his testimony.19

Enriquillo's Rebellion

The boy born Guarocuya was a nephew of Anacaona, a member of the royal family of Jaragua. His father, Magiocatex, was among the nitaínos burned alive in the caney during Ovando's massacre of 1503. Guarocuya survived because he was a child. He was taken in by Franciscan friars (some sources say Dominican), educated in a monastery, baptized, and given the name Enriquillo. He learned to read and write Spanish. He learned Spanish law. He would eventually use both against his teachers.20

As an adult, Enriquillo was assigned as an encomendado to a Spaniard named Andrés de Valenzuela. The relationship was abusive. Valenzuela seized Enriquillo's property, including a horse (a significant possession for an indigenous person under colonial rule). He then assaulted Enriquillo's wife, Mencía. Enriquillo sought redress through Spanish legal channels, as he had been taught to do. He appealed to the local magistrate, to the audiencia in Santo Domingo, and to the governor. Each appeal was dismissed or ignored. The law that the Dominicans had taught him to respect offered him nothing.21

In 1519, Enriquillo fled to the Bahoruco Mountains in the southwestern part of Hispaniola, the same rugged terrain that had once been part of the cacicazgo of Jaragua. He gathered followers: Taíno who had escaped or been released from encomiendas, and eventually African enslaved people who had fled Spanish plantations. Over the next fourteen years, he led a guerrilla resistance from the mountain forests. His knowledge of the terrain was exact. Spanish military expeditions sent to capture or kill him failed repeatedly. The Bahoruco's steep ravines, dense vegetation, and limited access points made conventional military operations impractical, and Enriquillo moved his camps constantly.22

The rebellion was not a mass uprising. Enriquillo's forces were small, probably never exceeding a few hundred people. But they could not be dislodged, and their continued existence was a political embarrassment to the colonial administration. Spain had conquered the Aztec and Inca empires; it could not suppress a band of survivors in a Caribbean mountain range. Multiple expeditions over more than a decade consumed resources and returned empty-handed.

In 1533, negotiations began. Las Casas, by then an influential figure in colonial debates, served as one of the intermediaries. The terms were remarkable: Spain agreed to let Enriquillo and his people live free in the Bahoruco, exempt from the encomienda. It was, as Samuel Wilson has noted, the only successful indigenous rebellion in the early colonial Caribbean.23 Enriquillo accepted. He settled with his community in the town of Boyá, in the foothills of the Bahoruco. He died there around 1535, roughly two years after the treaty. The cause is unrecorded.

In the Dominican Republic today, Enriquillo is a national hero. A lake in the Bahoruco region bears his name. The literary and political uses of his story have been extensive, from Manuel de Jesús Galván's 1882 novel Enriquillo to twentieth-century Dominican nationalism. These appropriations have sometimes smoothed the story into a simple narrative of indigenous nobility and Spanish villainy. The actual history is harder: a man raised by his family's killers, trained in their language and their law, who turned both against them when the law failed, and who won the only concession the colonial system ever granted to a Taíno leader.24

The Construction of Extinction

By the second half of the sixteenth century, Spanish administrative records on Hispaniola largely stopped distinguishing indigenous people from the mixed population. The colonial census categories that replaced earlier ethnic designations, "mestizo," "mulato," "pardo," absorbed Taíno descendants into a racial classification system that had no place for "indio" once the encomienda ceased to need the category. When the indigenous labor force was replaced by enslaved Africans (a process well underway by the 1520s), the administrative machinery lost interest in counting indigenous survivors.25

The disappearance from the records was not the same as disappearance from the land. Taíno and part-Taíno communities persisted in the mountainous interior of Hispaniola, in eastern Cuba, and in the hill country of Puerto Rico, areas where Spanish settlement was sparse and administrative oversight minimal. Lynne Guitar's research in the Dominican Republic has documented communities in the Cibao and the Samaná Peninsula that maintained indigenous identity, agricultural practices, and elements of Taíno language into the eighteenth century and beyond.26

The narrative of total extinction hardened in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. By then, it served multiple purposes. For colonial administrators, it eliminated the question of indigenous land rights: if no indigenous people existed, no claims could be made. For the emerging Creole elites of the Caribbean, it simplified racial categories into a binary of European and African, with the "vanished" Taíno available as a romantic, non-threatening symbol of national origin. The Taíno could be honored in poetry and place names precisely because they were understood to be gone. A living indigenous population would have complicated property arrangements, racial taxonomies, and national mythologies in ways that extinction conveniently foreclosed.27

The 1802 declaration of extinction by Spanish authorities was not a finding of fact. It was a legal and administrative act that reflected the categories the colonial state chose to use. The people it declared nonexistent did not comply with the declaration. Their genetic, cultural, and linguistic traces persisted in the Caribbean population, and the evidence for that persistence, genetic, ethnographic, and archival, is the subject of the chapter that follows.
