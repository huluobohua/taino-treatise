Chapter Three

Cosmology and Mythology

The Source: Pané's Relación

Nearly everything we know about Taíno mythology descends from a single document written by a single observer. Fray Ramón Pané, a Hieronymite friar from Catalonia, arrived in the Caribbean on Columbus's second voyage in 1493. Columbus himself commissioned Pané to live among the Taíno and record their beliefs, a task that the friar carried out over several years in the Macorís region of Hispaniola. By approximately 1498, Pané had completed a short treatise titled Relación acerca de las antigüedades de los indios, a work now recognized as the first ethnographic text written in the Americas.1

The importance of this document is difficult to overstate, and so are its limitations. Pané was not a trained scholar. His prose is often disorganized, circling back on itself, inserting clarifications mid-narrative in ways that suggest he was transcribing oral accounts without fully understanding their internal logic. He wrote from within the interpretive framework of late fifteenth-century Catholicism, and his descriptions of Taíno spiritual practices carry the assumptions of a man who regarded those practices as diabolical error. He calls zemís "idols." He frames behiques as servants of the devil. These impositions are systematic, not incidental, and any reading of Pané must account for them.2

The textual history compounds the problem. Pané's original manuscript, likely written in Castilian with Catalan inflections, does not survive. What we have are intermediary copies: an Italian translation by Alfonso de Ulloa, included in Fernando Colón's biography of his father, and a Spanish redaction by Bartolomé de las Casas, who paraphrased and rearranged the material in his Apologética historia sumaria. The modern critical edition by José Juan Arrom, published in 1974 and revised in 1999, attempts to reconstruct Pané's original from these secondary witnesses. Arrom's editorial apparatus, including his linguistic analysis of Taíno terms and his annotations drawing on archaeology and comparative Caribbean ethnography, transformed the Relación from a curiosity into a usable scholarly source.3

Even with Arrom's work, the Relación remains a fragmentary, mediated text. Pané recorded only what his Taíno informants chose to share, filtered through what he was able to comprehend, reshaped by the copyists who transmitted his words. The narratives below come from this single, compromised source. Where archaeology, linguistics, or comparative Arawakan ethnography can corroborate or complicate Pané's account, I note it. Where he is the only witness, I say so.

Emergence from the Caves

Taíno creation did not begin with a god shaping the world from nothing. It began underground. According to Pané, the ancestral Taíno emerged from a cave called Cacibajagua, located within a mountain called Cauta on the island of Hispaniola. A second cave, Amayaúna, gave rise to the other peoples of the world. The specificity is worth noting: origin is tied to a named place, a particular mountain, a particular island. This is not an abstract cosmogony but a territorial one, grounding the Taíno claim to Hispaniola in the literal bedrock of the land.4

The cave was not simply a point of exit. It was a guarded threshold. Pané tells us that a figure named Mácocael was assigned to stand watch at the mouth of Cacibajagua during the night, controlling when and how people could leave. One night, Mácocael failed to return to his post before dawn. The Sun caught him outside, and the punishment was transformation: Mácocael was turned to stone. This episode establishes a pattern that recurs throughout the emergence cycle. The Sun is not a benevolent deity in these narratives. It is a force of petrification and metamorphosis, dangerous to those who violate its temporal boundaries.5

Others who ventured out to fish were similarly caught and transformed, not into stone but into jobo trees (Spondias mombin, a fruit tree native to the Caribbean). Pané does not explain why the punishment differs, and scholars have speculated variously. Sebastián Robiou Lamarche reads the sequence as an etiological narrative: the Taíno are explaining the origin of particular geological and botanical features, the standing stones and distinctive trees of their homeland, by incorporating them into the story of first emergence. The natural world is not backdrop; it is populated by transformed ancestors.6

Separately, Pané records that the Sun and the Moon themselves emerged from a cave called Iguanaboina. This detail is brief, almost parenthetical in his account, but its implications are significant. If the celestial bodies also originated from within the earth, then the entire cosmos shares the same subterranean origin as humanity. The caves are not merely human birthplaces but the source of the world's fundamental structure: light, darkness, and the cycles of day and night.

Deminán Caracaracol and the Birth of the Sea

The most complex narrative Pané preserves centers on a figure called Deminán Caracaracol, the first and most prominent of four brothers, quadruplets born of a single mother who died in childbirth. The number four is structurally important. It recurs in Taíno art and ceremony, and the quadruplet motif appears in other Arawakan cosmologies as well, suggesting a shared inheritance within the broader language family.7

The four brothers learned of a great gourd (higüera) that belonged to Yaya, a figure Pané identifies as the Original Spirit or supreme being. Inside this gourd were the bones of Yayael, Yaya's son, whom Yaya had killed after Yayael plotted against him. When Yaya placed his son's bones in the gourd, they transformed into fish. The gourd, then, contained not merely remains but the entire potential of the sea: its water, its creatures, its sustaining abundance. Yaya and his wife would periodically eat from the gourd, consuming the fish that regenerated from the bones.8

The four brothers, driven by hunger, attempted to take the gourd while Yaya was away. When Deminán lifted it, the vessel fell and shattered, and from it poured an immense quantity of water filled with fish. The water spread across the land and became the Caribbean Sea. The islands of the Antilles are, in this telling, the remnants of higher ground left exposed by the flood. Arrom and others have noted the elegance of this cosmogonic structure: a single act of transgression, a gourd dropped by a trickster-hero, generates the entire marine geography that defined Taíno life. The sea is simultaneously a gift and a consequence of disobedience. The fish are both food and the transformed remains of a murdered son.9

Deminán's story does not end with the flood. Fleeing from a grandfather spirit named Bayamanaco, Deminán begged for cassava bread. Bayamanaco, angered, blew cohoba powder (a hallucinogenic snuff) onto Deminán's back. The powder caused a massive swelling. His brothers attempted to cut the growth open, and from it emerged a living female turtle (caguama). The brothers took the turtle and built their dwelling upon its back. From the turtle-woman, Pané tells us, humanity descended.10

The density of this narrative rewards careful attention. It combines an origin of the sea, an origin of the islands, an origin of cassava (through Bayamanaco), and an origin of humanity through the turtle, all woven into a single sequence of events involving the same four brothers. The turtle as generative mother resonates with broader Amerindian cosmology; turtle-earth and turtle-origin myths appear across the Americas, from the Haudenosaunee to the Wayúu. Whether these reflect ancient shared roots or parallel invention remains an open and probably unanswerable question.11

The Cemí Concept

The word zemí (also written cemí) appears constantly in Pané's text and across the archaeological literature, but its meaning is frequently misunderstood. Zemí does not refer to an object. It refers to an immaterial force, a spiritual quality or vital energy associated with deities, ancestors, and certain aspects of the natural world. José Oliver, in his extensive analysis of three-pointed stones, has argued that zemí denotes a power that can be concentrated, housed, and channeled but never reduced to the physical form that contains it. The carved stone, the cotton figure, the wooden idol: these are vessels for zemí, not the zemí itself.12

Arrom traced the linguistic roots of the word and suggested a connection to a quality akin to "sweetness" or "essence," a reading that, if correct, positions zemí as something closer to the Polynesian concept of mana or the Algonquian manitou than to the Christian notion of an idol representing a false god. Pané uses the word interchangeably for the spiritual force and for the objects that embody it, a conflation that has confused centuries of readers. Modern scholars maintain the distinction carefully.13

Oliver identified four major categories of zemí sculptures in the archaeological record. Three-pointed stones (trigonolitos) are the most numerous; they are conical or triangular forms, sometimes plain, sometimes carved with elaborate anthropomorphic or zoomorphic imagery, found throughout the Greater Antilles and often recovered from agricultural contexts, suggesting a connection to soil fertility and the cassava cycle. Large stone heads, sometimes with elaborate hairstyles or dual faces, appear to represent specific ancestral figures or deities. Stone collars (yugos) and elbow stones (piedras de codo) are more enigmatic; their function has been debated, but they are most frequently associated with the batey (ball game) and with ceremonial contexts. All four forms share a common purpose: they concentrate and make accessible the zemí force that animates the Taíno spiritual world.14

The Behique and the Cohoba Ritual

Access to the zemí world was not casual. It was mediated by the behique, a figure who combined the roles of priest, healer, diviner, and keeper of oral tradition. The behique underwent rigorous training and fasting. Pané describes their healing practices, including the sucking of illness from the body and the use of medicinal plants, but he frames these activities within his own understanding of demonic deception. Stripped of that framing, the behique emerges as a specialist in altered states of consciousness, a mediator between the visible and invisible dimensions of Taíno reality.15

The central mechanism of this mediation was the cohoba ceremony. Cohoba was prepared from the ground seeds of the cojóbana tree (Anadenanthera peregrina), a leguminous plant whose seeds contain the psychoactive compounds dimethyltryptamine (DMT) and bufotenin. The powder was inhaled through Y-shaped snuff tubes, many examples of which survive in the archaeological record, carved from bone, wood, or stone, often decorated with zemí imagery. The tubes themselves were ritual objects, not mere tools.16

Pané and other chroniclers describe the ceremony in enough detail to reconstruct its basic sequence. Participants, who included the cacique, the nitaínos (secondary chiefs), and the behiques, first purged their bodies by inserting carved vomiting spatulas (espátulas vómicas) into their throats. This purification was both physical and symbolic: the body had to be emptied before it could receive the zemí's communication. The cohoba powder was then placed on a flat ceremonial platform and inhaled through the bifurcated tubes. The resulting trance state, which the chroniclers describe as involving visual hallucinations, disorientation, and copious nasal discharge, was understood not as intoxication but as a genuine encounter with the zemí spirits.17

The purpose of the ceremony was practical as much as spiritual. The cacique and behiques sought guidance from the zemís on matters of community governance: whether to wage war, when to plant, how to treat illness, what the future held. Cohoba was a technology of decision-making, a means of consulting a wisdom that the Taíno believed resided outside ordinary human perception. The ceremony took place in the caney (the cacique's house) or in the central plaza, and it was a collective, public event, not a private mystical experience.18

The Deities

Yúcahu Bagua Maórocoti

The principal male deity of the Taíno was Yúcahu Bagua Maórocoti, a name that Arrom parsed as "Spirit of cassava, of the sea, without a male ancestor." The name itself is a theological statement. Yúcahu is lord of the two resources that sustained Taíno life: cassava, the staple crop grown in mounded conucos, and the sea, the source of fish and the medium of inter-island travel. The final element of his name, Maórocoti, indicates that he had no father; he was born of his mother alone. This is not a minor genealogical detail. It establishes a cosmological principle: the supreme male force of fertility and sustenance originates from a female source without male intervention.19

Atabey

That female source is Atabey (also rendered Atabei or Atabeyra), the supreme goddess. Pané identifies her as the mother of Yúcahu and associates her with fresh water, the moon, fertility, and childbirth. She is, in the Taíno cosmological hierarchy, the foundational power. Where Yúcahu governs the products of the living world, Atabey governs the conditions that make that world possible: the rain that feeds the rivers, the lunar cycles that regulate planting and menstruation, the biological capacity of women to bear children. She has five names in Pané's account, each associated with a different aspect of her power, suggesting that what Pané recorded as a single deity may have been a composite of several overlapping female spiritual forces.20

The Storm Triad: Guabancex, Guatauba, Coatrisquie

Among Atabey's manifestations, or perhaps among her subordinate spirits, stands a triad that the Taíno used to explain the most destructive force in their environment: the hurricane. Guabancex was the goddess of storms, the hurricane itself rendered as a divine personality. Pané describes her as terrifying. She did not act alone. Guatauba served as her herald, the spirit who marshaled the winds and thunder that announce the storm's approach. Coatrisquie was the spirit of floodwaters, the one who unleashed the torrential rains and surging rivers that accompany a hurricane's passage over land.21

This triad is worth pausing over. The Taíno lived in one of the most hurricane-prone regions on earth, and these storms, arriving annually between June and November, could annihilate crops, destroy villages, and kill in large numbers. The storm triad is a meteorological theory expressed as theology. It decomposes the hurricane into three functional phases: the wind and electrical disturbance (Guabancex), the atmospheric warning signs (Guatauba), and the hydrological destruction (Coatrisquie). Each phase is personified, given agency, and placed within a hierarchical relationship. The Taíno were not simply afraid of hurricanes. They had analyzed them, identified their constituent processes, and organized that analysis into a coherent spiritual framework. The word "hurricane" itself derives from the Taíno hurakán, a linguistic legacy that persists in every Atlantic language.22

The Tripartite Cosmos

Behind the individual myths and deities lies a broader cosmological structure that organized Taíno reality into three domains. The first was the world of the living, the visible domain of villages, conucos, rivers, and forests. The second was the spirit realm, accessible through cohoba and through the zemí objects that served as points of contact between human and spiritual reality. The third was Coaybay, the land of the dead.23

Coaybay was not an underworld in the sense that European readers might assume. It was not a place of punishment. Pané describes it as a shadow-country, a domain that overlapped geographically with the world of the living but belonged to the night. Coaybay was ruled by a lord named Maquetaurie Guayaba, whose name connects him to the guava fruit. The spirits of the dead, called opía, resided there during the day and emerged at night. Their preferred form was that of the bat, a nocturnal creature already invested with symbolic meaning across Mesoamerican and Caribbean cultures. At night, the opía could also take human form and visit the living, sometimes to comfort, sometimes to deceive.24

Pané records that the living could distinguish an opía in human form from a real person by a specific physical marker: the opía had no navel. The absence of a navel signifies the absence of birth, a body that was never connected to a mother, never gestated, never delivered into the world through the ordinary biological process. The detail is small but conceptually precise. It draws a boundary between the living and the dead that is rooted in the body itself, in the visible trace of human origin.

The opía ate guava fruit, a detail that Robiou Lamarche connects to broader Caribbean and South American traditions associating specific fruits with the dead. The night was the domain of the opía; the day belonged to the living. This temporal division maps onto the cave narratives discussed earlier, where the Sun's light is dangerous, transformative, and punitive. The cosmos divides not only spatially (above, below, and between) but temporally, with night and day serving as distinct ontological zones governed by different beings and different rules. The living inhabit the sunlit hours. The dead claim the darkness, not as a punishment, but as their proper element.25

Interpreting the Fragments

Any reconstruction of Taíno cosmology must reckon with what has been lost. Pané recorded only what he heard in one region of Hispaniola, from informants whose names he sometimes gives and sometimes does not, during a period when the Taíno world was already under catastrophic pressure from colonization. The myths he preserved may have varied significantly from island to island, from cacicazgo to cacicazgo. We have no way of knowing what the Taíno of Puerto Rico, Jamaica, or Cuba told about the caves of origin, or whether Deminán's story circulated beyond Hispaniola. The silence of the archaeological record on matters of narrative (stone and pottery preserve form, not words) means that Pané's text stands almost alone.26

Comparative approaches offer partial remedies. Samuel Wilson and others have drawn on the mythologies of mainland Arawakan peoples, particularly the Lokono and Wayúu, to contextualize Taíno narratives within a broader family of stories. Shared motifs (the turtle-woman, the gourd of origin, the cave of emergence) suggest deep continuities within the Arawakan diaspora, but the specific forms these motifs take in each culture differ considerably. The comparative method illuminates; it does not fill in the blanks.27

What the surviving material does reveal is a cosmology of considerable internal coherence. The Taíno world was animated by zemí, a spiritual force that pervaded the natural environment and could be concentrated in specific objects and beings. Humanity emerged from the earth through caves and remained connected to the earth through agriculture, through the burial of three-pointed stones in conucos, through the return of the dead to Coaybay. The sea was born from an act of sacred mischief. Storms were not random destruction but the work of a divine family whose members could be named, addressed, and perhaps propitiated. Day and night were not simply periods of light and darkness but distinct cosmological territories, each with its own inhabitants and its own logic.

Pané, for all his limitations, captured enough of this system for later scholars to perceive its architecture. Arrom's editorial work, Oliver's archaeological analyses, Robiou Lamarche's comparative mythology, and Wilson's ethnohistorical synthesis have together produced a picture that, while incomplete, is intellectually serious and internally consistent. The Taíno cosmology that emerges from this collective scholarship is not a collection of quaint legends. It is a systematic attempt to explain the origin and structure of the world, the relationship between the living and the dead, the causes of natural phenomena, and the proper means of accessing wisdom that lies beyond ordinary perception. That we have it at all is due to a Catalan friar who was told to write down what he heard, and who did so imperfectly, but did so.
