Chapter Four

Material Culture

A civilization can be read through what it makes. The Taíno left no written language, no codex, no inscribed stele; the colonial encounter destroyed most of the perishable record within decades. What survives is stone, ceramic, bone, shell, and the words Spanish chroniclers transcribed, sometimes accurately, sometimes not. From these fragments, a coherent material world emerges: one organized around the processing of a toxic root crop, shaped by long-distance canoe travel, and expressed through objects whose aesthetic vocabulary was inseparable from religious meaning.

The Cassava Complex

Yuca, known in English as cassava or manioc (Manihot esculenta), was the foundation of Taíno subsistence. The Taíno cultivated two varieties: sweet yuca, which could be peeled and eaten after simple roasting or boiling, and bitter yuca, which contains high concentrations of cyanogenic glucosides and requires elaborate processing to render it safe for consumption.1 Bitter yuca yields more starch per hectare than the sweet variety, so despite the added labor, it was the preferred crop for large populations. The technology developed to process it was among the most sophisticated food-preparation systems in the precolonial Americas.

Processing began with harvesting the tuberous roots from conucos, purpose-built earth mounds that improved drainage, concentrated compost, and allowed intercropping of several species. The roots were peeled and grated on boards inset with coral fragments or rough stone chips. The resulting pulp, still saturated with toxic hydrocyanic acid, was packed into a cibucán, a long woven tube of palm fiber that functioned as a press. Suspended from a beam and weighted at the bottom (sometimes by a woman sitting on a stick threaded through the lower loop), the cibucán squeezed the poisonous juice from the pulp over hours. Oviedo described the device in detail and marveled at its efficiency.2

The extracted liquid was not wasted. Left to settle, the starch separated from the cyanide-bearing water; the clear liquid on top could be boiled down (heat destroying the remaining toxins) to produce a pepper-spiked condiment. The pressed pulp, now safe, was spread onto a burén, a large flat clay griddle placed over a fire, and toasted into thin round cakes. The resulting casabe (cassava bread) was light, dry, and remarkably durable. Spanish sailors quickly recognized its value: casabe could last months without spoiling, superior to wheat bread for Caribbean voyages.3

The burén is one of the most diagnostic artifacts in Caribbean archaeology. These flat ceramic griddles, typically 40 to 60 centimeters in diameter, appear at archaeological sites across the Greater Antilles wherever Taíno settlement is confirmed. Their presence in the stratigraphic record marks the arrival of cassava-cultivating peoples as clearly as any pottery style.4

Cassava was not the only crop. The Taíno grew batata (sweet potato, Ipomoea batatas), which Columbus brought back to Spain, where it became patata and eventually "potato" in English (transferred later to the Andean tuber). They cultivated maíz (corn), though it held a secondary role compared to yuca. Ají (chili pepper, Capsicum spp.) seasoned nearly everything. Tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) was grown for ceremonial use, its smoke integral to cohoba rituals and healing practices. The conuco system supported all of these crops simultaneously, the mounded beds functioning as polyculture gardens.

Pottery and Periodization

Caribbean archaeologists have long used pottery styles as chronological markers, and the Taíno ceramic sequence records roughly two thousand years of changing aesthetics, trade networks, and cultural priorities. Irving Rouse's classification system, developed across several decades of fieldwork beginning in the 1930s, remains the standard framework, though subsequent scholars (particularly Keegan and Hofman) have refined its categories and questioned some of its assumptions about cultural continuity.5

The earliest relevant tradition is the Saladoid series, named after the Saladero site on the lower Orinoco in Venezuela. Saladoid pottery, which appears in the Lesser Antilles and Puerto Rico from roughly 500 BCE onward, is technically accomplished: thin-walled vessels with finely executed white-on-red (W-O-R) painted designs, often featuring curvilinear motifs and small zoomorphic adornments (modeled animal heads, frogs, bats) affixed to vessel rims or handles. The craftsmanship suggests specialized potters working within a tradition that valued precision and decorative elaboration.6

Over the following centuries, as Saladoid communities expanded westward through the Greater Antilles, pottery styles shifted. The Ostionoid series (named after the Ostiones site in western Puerto Rico, dating from roughly 600 CE) marks a turning point: painted decoration becomes less common, vessel walls thicken, and the emphasis moves from surface painting to three-dimensional form. Handles grow larger and more sculptural. Incised designs replace painted ones. Whether this shift reflects changing ritual practices, the loss of specific trade pigments, population growth, or some combination remains debated.7

By the Classic Taíno period (roughly 1200 to 1500 CE), pottery had divided into two categories: utilitarian cooking and storage vessels, and ceremonial pieces of considerable artistry. The utilitarian wares are plain, functional, abundant. The ceremonial pieces are something else. Effigy vessels depict mythological scenes drawn from the same narrative cycle Pané recorded in his Relación. One striking example is the Deminán Caracaracol vessel at the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian (catalog number 053753), collected by Theodore de Booy during fieldwork in the Dominican Republic around 1916.8 It depicts the mythological hero Deminán, one of four sons of Itiba Cahubaba, whose back swelled into a turtle after being spat upon by the spirit Bayamanaco. From that turtle-shell growth, according to Pané, emerged the first woman. The vessel captures the transformation: a human figure with an unmistakable hump on its back, rendered in the compressed symbolic style of Classic Taíno ceramic art.

Taíno potters used natural dyes to finish and decorate their work. Jagua (Genipa americana) produced a blue-black dye that was also applied to the body for ceremonial occasions. Annatto (Bixa orellana), called bija by the Taíno, yielded a red-orange pigment used on both pottery and skin. These same plant dyes served double duty across material culture: body paint, pottery decoration, cotton dyeing, and possibly wood finishing all drew from the same botanical palette.

Cotton, Textiles, and the Hamaca

The Taíno cultivated cotton (Gossypium spp.) and spun it into thread for weaving. Direct evidence of Taíno textiles is scarce, since organic fibers rarely survive in tropical archaeological contexts, but Spanish accounts confirm the production of woven cloth, belts, and bands. The most significant textile product was the hamaca, the suspended sleeping net or bed that Europeans encountered for the first time in the Caribbean. Columbus noted hamacas in his journal after landing in the Bahamas in October 1492; within decades, the word had entered Spanish as hamaca and eventually passed into English as "hammock."9

The hamaca was not simply a bed. It was the primary piece of domestic furniture. Taíno bohíos (houses) were organized around hamacas strung from the interior posts, and a person's hamaca was carried when traveling. Las Casas describes caciques receiving visitors while reclining in their hamacas, and the practice of burying the dead in a hamaca is attested at several sites. The word's passage from Taíno to global languages is one of the clearest cases of Caribbean linguistic survival: "hammock" is used in dozens of languages today, and most speakers have no idea they are using a Taíno word.

Canoes

The canoa is another Taíno word that colonized the colonizers' languages. Taíno canoes were dugouts, carved from single tree trunks, sometimes from the massive ceiba (Ceiba pentandra), the sacred tree that in Taíno cosmology connected the worlds of the living and the dead. The largest canoes could carry forty to fifty or more people and were used for inter-island trade, diplomatic missions, fishing expeditions, and raiding parties.10

Building a large canoa was a communal project. The selected tree was felled, its trunk hollowed by controlled burning and adze work, and the hull shaped over weeks or months. Columbus described Taíno canoes during his first voyage, noting that some were large enough to carry seventy or eighty men, though these numbers may reflect the exaggeration common in early contact narratives. Peter Martyr d'Anghiera reported canoes carrying merchandise between islands separated by open-ocean crossings of forty miles or more.11 The archaeological record confirms this: Taíno-style artifacts and raw materials (jadeitite, flint, specific clays) appear distributed across islands that required significant open-water voyaging to reach. Canoes were the infrastructure of Taíno civilization; without them, the cacicazgos could not have maintained the trade relationships and political alliances that defined their social order.

Duhos

The duho was a low ceremonial seat, typically carved from a single piece of wood (guayacán, or lignum vitae, was preferred for its hardness and dark color) or, less commonly, from stone. Duhos sat close to the ground on four short legs, with a curved back that often terminated in a carved head, sometimes human, sometimes animal, sometimes a composite being that resists easy classification. The seated figure leaned slightly backward, a posture that may have facilitated the trance states induced during cohoba ceremonies.12

Duhos were objects of authority. Only caciques and behiques (shamanic healers) used them during public rituals. To sit on a duho was to claim a specific social position; the object physically distinguished its occupant from everyone else in the batey, where others sat on the ground or stood. Several duhos survive in museum collections, including examples at the British Museum and the Museo del Hombre Dominicano. The finest show intricate carving: inlaid eyes of shell or gold, skeletal rib motifs on the seat backs, and surface textures suggesting the duho itself was understood as a container for cemí (spirit force) in the same way a stone zemí could house a deity.13

José Oliver has argued that the duho functioned as a cosmological instrument, positioning the cacique between human and spirit worlds during the cohoba ritual. Seated on the duho, the cacique ingested cohoba (a hallucinogenic snuff prepared from Anadenanthera peregrina seeds), purged through a vomiting spatula, and entered a visionary state in which he communicated with zemís. The duho was not merely furniture; it was part of the ritual apparatus, as essential to the ceremony as the cohoba itself.14

Gold, Guanín, and the Aesthetic of Brilliance

The Taíno did not mine gold on any large scale. They obtained it through river panning and through trade networks connecting the Greater Antilles to the South American mainland, where goldworking traditions were far more developed. Gold in its pure state interested the Taíno less than guanín, an alloy of approximately 85 percent copper with smaller proportions of gold and silver. The preference baffled the Spanish and set in motion one of the most consequential misunderstandings of the colonial encounter.15

Guanín was valued for sensory properties that Europeans either did not notice or did not consider important. Its polished surface produced a warm, reddish shine distinct from the cooler gleam of pure gold. Oliver has documented what he calls an "aesthetic of brilliance" in Taíno culture: a systematic association between light-reflecting surfaces and spiritual power.16 Objects that caught and returned light (polished stone, shell, guanín, certain seeds) were understood to possess or channel cemí. Guanín also had a distinctive smell when rubbed, which the Taíno compared to the plant taguagua. This olfactory quality mattered; in a culture that did not privilege sight as absolutely as post-Enlightenment Europe did, the smell of a metal was a legitimate marker of value.

Caciques wore guanín medallions as markers of rank. Chief Bohechío of Xaragua, one of the five paramount caciques of Hispaniola at contact, carried the epithet Tureywa Hobin: "king as dazzling and heavenly as guanín."17 The title fused political authority with spiritual connotation: to be like guanín was to reflect supernatural light, to be a conduit between the human world and the cemís.

When Columbus arrived, he was looking for gold. The Taíno offered guanín freely, not understanding that the Spanish valued the gold content and dismissed the copper. The Spanish could not comprehend why the Taíno would alloy precious gold with base copper, reducing its purity. Each side misread the other completely. The Spanish saw naive primitives who did not understand the worth of gold. The Taíno may well have seen baffling strangers who rejected the superior material (guanín, with its warm glow, its smell, its spiritual charge) in favor of a cold, odorless, inert metal. This mutual incomprehension accelerated the catastrophe: the Spanish demand for gold tribute, forced labor in mines and rivers, demographic collapse. The material culture of metallurgy became, within years, a mechanism of destruction.18

Ball Courts and Civic Space

The batey was a rectangular or oval plaza, bounded by upright stones or earthen embankments, that served as game court, civic gathering place, and ritual space. The word referred both to the physical court and to the ball game played within it: a sport involving a rubber ball (made from the latex of Hevea or Castilla trees) struck with hips, shoulders, and knees but not hands.19 Matches could settle disputes between communities, mark seasonal transitions, or accompany a cacique's funerary rites.

The stone alignments bordering bateyes were sometimes carved with petroglyphs: faces, geometric designs, images of cemís. These carvings transformed the court into a charged space, surrounded by spirit presences that watched and presumably influenced the outcome of the game.

The largest known ceremonial site in the Caribbean is Caguana (also called Capá) in the municipality of Utuado, in the mountainous interior of Puerto Rico. Caguana contains ten stone-lined courts of varying sizes, arranged along the banks of the Río Tanamá, the largest measuring approximately 50 by 35 meters. The boundary stones bear some of the finest petroglyphs in the Taíno world, including a famous image interpreted as Atabey, the mother goddess.20 Rouse dated the site's primary occupation to approximately 1200 to 1500 CE. Caguana was almost certainly a regional ceremonial center where communities from surrounding valleys gathered for areytos (ceremonial dances and songs), ball games, and political councils. The labor required to construct ten stone-lined courts with elaborate petroglyphs indicates a level of social organization that contradicts any characterization of the Taíno as "simple" island peoples.

Bateyes have been identified at sites across Puerto Rico, Hispaniola, Jamaica, and eastern Cuba. Their distribution maps onto the territory of the Classic Taíno cacicazgos, and their presence at a site indicates a community of significant size and political importance. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian, and the Museo del Hombre Dominicano all hold collections drawn from batey sites and their surrounding ceremonial precincts, including stone collars (yugos), elbow stones, and three-pointed zemís that may have been used in ball-court rituals or placed as offerings within the court boundaries.21

Taken whole, the material record describes a civilization that was technologically inventive, aesthetically sophisticated, and deeply integrated across its island chain. The cassava complex alone represents a feat of applied chemistry: the systematic detoxification of a poisonous plant into a storable staple food using purpose-built tools that formed an interlocking system. Canoes connected islands across hundreds of miles of open water. The guanín trade linked the Antilles to South America. Ball courts provided the physical infrastructure for a political and ceremonial life that was anything but simple. What survives in museums and archaeological sites is a fraction of what existed before 1492. Even that fraction is enough to reconstruct a world.
