Chapter One

Origins and Migration

The people whom Columbus encountered on Guanahaní in October 1492 had arrived there by a route stretching more than three thousand kilometers, back through the arc of the Lesser Antilles and into the river systems of northern South America. Their ancestors had left the middle Orinoco basin centuries earlier, carrying with them a ceramic tradition, an agricultural system centered on bitter cassava, and a language belonging to the Arawak family. By the time Europeans recorded them, their descendants occupied every major island in the Greater Antilles and dozens of smaller ones in the Bahamas. The reconstruction of that migration, its timing, its stages, and its cultural transformations, has been the central project of Caribbean archaeology for more than a century.

The Arawak Linguistic World

The Taíno spoke a language (or closely related group of languages) within the Arawak family, one of the most geographically widespread language families in the Americas. At European contact, Arawakan-speaking peoples lived from the Peruvian Amazon to the Bahamas, a range exceeded in the Western Hemisphere only by the Tupí-Guaraní family.1 The northern branch of Arawak, to which Taíno belonged, had its deepest roots in the lowland forests between the Orinoco and Amazon rivers. Linguistic reconstruction suggests that the proto-Arawakan homeland lay somewhere in the western Amazon basin; a northward and eastward expansion carried Arawakan speakers to the Orinoco delta and the coast of Venezuela, from which the island migration began.2

No Taíno texts survive. The language is reconstructed from Spanish transcriptions of place names, personal names, and the handful of Taíno words that entered Caribbean Spanish and, through it, global vocabulary: canoa, hamaca, huracán, tabaco, barbacoa, maíz. These survivals, while fragmentary, confirm the Arawakan affiliation beyond serious dispute. They also indicate regional variation; the Lucayan Taíno of the Bahamas, the Classic Taíno of Hispaniola, and the communities of eastern Cuba appear to have spoken distinguishable but mutually intelligible dialects.3

First Peoples: The Lithic and Archaic Ages

The Taíno were not the first inhabitants of the Caribbean. Irving Rouse, whose chronological framework still structures most archaeological discussion of the region, identified two earlier population movements into the islands.4 The first, which he assigned to the Lithic Age, brought settlers from the Yucatán Peninsula or Central America into Cuba and western Hispaniola around 4000 BC. These people left behind crude flaked-stone tools and few other traces; their identity, language, and fate remain largely unknown.

The second pre-ceramic movement came from the opposite direction. Beginning around 2000 BC, peoples from the South American mainland moved northward through the Lesser Antilles into Puerto Rico. Rouse classified these as Archaic Age populations. They were foragers, relying on shellfish, fish, iguanas, and wild plants rather than agriculture. Their stone tool assemblages were more refined than those of the Lithic Age inhabitants, including ground-stone axes and rudimentary mortars. On Puerto Rico and in parts of Hispaniola, Archaic Age sites are numerous enough to suggest substantial populations that persisted for millennia before the arrival of ceramic-using, agricultural peoples.5

The relationship between these Archaic populations and the later Taíno is unclear. Some scholars have argued for absorption and admixture; others hold that the Archaic peoples were displaced or died out before the Saladoid expansion reached the Greater Antilles. Ancient DNA evidence has begun to address the question but has not resolved it. What is certain is that the Archaic inhabitants were culturally distinct from the Arawakan migrants who followed them, and that the ceramic boundary between the two is sharp in the archaeological record.

The Saladoid Expansion

The migration that produced the Taíno began with a ceramic tradition named after the site of Saladero, on the lower Orinoco in eastern Venezuela. First identified by the Venezuelan archaeologists José Maria Cruxent and Irving Rouse in the 1940s and 1950s, Saladoid pottery is characterized by white-on-red painting, zone-incised crosshatching (known as ZIC decoration), and small modeled-incised lugs depicting animal and human faces.6 The vessels were well-made, thin-walled, and often elaborately decorated. Saladoid communities also produced polished stone pendants, shell ornaments, and worked bone, indicating a material culture considerably more complex than that of the Archaic populations they encountered in the islands.

Around 500 BC, Saladoid peoples began moving from the South American coast into the Lesser Antilles. The expansion was neither instantaneous nor uniform. The earliest Saladoid sites in the islands appear on Trinidad and Tobago, closest to the mainland, and progressively later dates are found moving northward through the Windward and Leeward Islands. By roughly 400 BC, Saladoid settlers had reached Puerto Rico, where sites like Hacienda Grande on the northeast coast demonstrate the full suite of Saladoid material culture in an island setting.7

The Saladoid expansion stalled in Puerto Rico. For reasons that remain debated, Saladoid peoples did not immediately continue westward into Hispaniola. The channel separating the two islands, the Mona Passage, is only about 120 kilometers wide, and Archaic Age populations had crossed it long before. The barrier was likely cultural rather than geographical. Corinne Hofman and others have suggested that dense Archaic populations on Hispaniola may have resisted or discouraged Saladoid settlement, or that the ecological transition from the smaller, drier eastern islands to the larger land masses of the Greater Antilles required adaptive changes that took time to develop.8

Saladoid culture in the islands was not static. Over the centuries between roughly 500 BC and AD 600, the distinctive white-on-red painted pottery gradually gave way to simpler decorative styles. Vessel forms changed. Settlement patterns shifted. Archaeologists have labeled this transitional period the "post-Saladoid" or "modified Saladoid," depending on the island and the classificatory preferences of the researcher. The terminology is cumbersome, but the underlying process is clear: the transplanted mainland culture was adapting to Caribbean conditions and diverging from its South American parent tradition.

The Ostionoid Development

Around AD 600, a new ceramic complex appeared in western Puerto Rico, centered on sites near the town of Ostiones. Rouse named this the Ostionoid series and considered it the first distinctly Caribbean (as opposed to transplanted South American) cultural tradition.9 Ostionoid pottery was coarser and less elaborately decorated than Saladoid ware, with thicker walls, less painting, and a greater reliance on incised and appliqué decoration. The shift was not a decline in skill so much as a change in priorities: vessel forms became more standardized, suggesting increasing specialization in food processing, particularly the preparation of cassava.

Ostionoid culture, unlike Saladoid, did cross the Mona Passage. By roughly AD 600 to 800, Ostionoid sites appear in eastern Hispaniola, and by AD 900 the complex had spread across the entire island and into eastern Cuba and Jamaica. This expansion coincided with, and perhaps drove, larger and more hierarchically organized settlements. Ball courts (known as bateyes), plazas bounded by stone alignments, and ceremonial objects including stone zemís begin to appear in the archaeological record during the Ostionoid period. The foundations of the chiefdom-level society that Columbus encountered were being laid.10

Rouse subdivided the Ostionoid series into regional substyles: Elenan Ostionoid in the Virgin Islands and eastern Puerto Rico, Ostionan in western Puerto Rico and eastern Hispaniola, Meillacoid across much of Hispaniola, and Chicoid, the latest and most elaborate expression, in central and eastern Hispaniola and parts of Puerto Rico. The Chicoid substyle, dating from roughly AD 1200 to the contact period, corresponds to what scholars generally mean by "Classic Taíno" culture: large cacicazgos (chiefdoms), monumental ball courts, elaborate stone collar belts (yugos), three-pointed zemís (trigonolitos), cotton weaving, and gold-copper alloy (guanín) ornaments.11

Settlement of the Bahamas

The Lucayan Taíno of the Bahamas represent the last major stage in the Arawakan expansion through the Caribbean, and their settlement chronology has been the subject of sustained debate. William Keegan, who has done more than any other scholar to reconstruct Lucayan prehistory, argued that the Bahamas were settled around AD 700 to 900, most likely from Hispaniola or eastern Cuba via the Great Inagua route.12 Great Inagua, the southernmost large island in the Bahamas, lies only about 90 kilometers from the northeastern tip of Cuba and is visible from the Haitian coast on clear days. Keegan proposed that settlers moved from Great Inagua northwestward through the Bahamas chain, island-hopping in a process that may have taken two or three centuries.

Keegan's model was not universally accepted. Some researchers favored a direct crossing from the Turks and Caicos to the central Bahamas; others argued for multiple founding events from different source populations. The title of Keegan's major work on the subject, The People Who Discovered Columbus (1992), captured both his emphasis on Lucayan agency and his interest in challenging the conventional framing of the 1492 encounter.13 Regardless of the precise route, the Bahamas appear to have been the last Caribbean islands settled by Arawakan-speaking peoples, and the Lucayan sites are uniformly late, postdating AD 700 and in many cases postdating AD 1000.

The Lucayan Taíno adapted to an environment markedly different from the mountainous Greater Antilles. The Bahamas are low-lying, dry, and poor in surface freshwater. Lucayan sites tend to be smaller than contemporaneous sites on Hispaniola or Puerto Rico, and the material culture, while recognizably Taíno, is less elaborate. Lucayan pottery is generally plain or simply decorated; stone zemís are rarer. The economy relied heavily on marine resources, supplemented by small-scale cultivation of cassava and other crops in soils that were shallow and infertile by Greater Antillean standards. These constraints produced a Taíno society that was less hierarchical and less materially complex than the Classic Taíno chiefdoms of Hispaniola, though the social and spiritual foundations were shared.14

The Genome Confirms the Migration

In 2018, a team led by Hannes Schroeder published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences the first ancient genome from a precolonial Caribbean individual: a Lucayan woman who lived on the island of Eleuthera approximately one thousand years ago. The genomic analysis demonstrated that her closest living genetic relatives are present-day Arawakan-speaking communities in northern South America, particularly in Venezuela and Colombia.15 This result independently confirmed, through a completely different line of evidence, the migration trajectory that linguists and archaeologists had reconstructed from language classification and ceramic typology. The Orinoco-to-Antilles route, inferred from pottery styles and word lists, was now written in nucleotide sequences.

The Schroeder study also addressed a persistent misconception. The Taíno had long been described as "extinct," a characterization rooted in colonial-era population collapse and reinforced by nineteenth- and twentieth-century racial categories that denied the survival of indigenous Caribbean peoples. The ancient DNA data showed genetic continuity between the precolonial Lucayan population and living communities in the Caribbean and northern South America, complicating the extinction narrative in ways that later chapters of this treatise will examine more fully.16

From Saladoid to Taíno: A Summary of Transformations

The trajectory from the Orinoco basin to the Classic Taíno polities of AD 1200 spans roughly seventeen centuries and involves a series of cultural transformations that can be tracked, with varying degrees of confidence, through the ceramic record. Saladoid colonists brought a lowland South American way of life into the islands: cassava agriculture, elaborate painted pottery, polished stone work, long-distance exchange networks. Over the first millennium AD, that transplanted culture adapted to island ecologies, absorbed or displaced earlier Archaic populations, and began developing distinctive Caribbean forms. The Ostionoid emergence around AD 600 marks the point at which the island cultures ceased to be provincial extensions of a mainland tradition and became something new. Over the next six centuries, increasing population density, the elaboration of chieftainship, the formalization of the batey and the ceremonial plaza, and the proliferation of zemí objects produced the civilization that the Spanish encountered.

This summary is necessarily schematic. The ceramic chronology, for all its utility, conceals enormous regional variation and imposes tidy boundaries on what were continuous processes. On any given island at any given time, the local situation was more complicated than the sequence Saladoid, Ostionoid, Chicoid suggests. Hofman and her collaborators have increasingly emphasized mobility, exchange, and interaction across the island chain, arguing that the Caribbean was a connected space rather than a set of isolated stepping stones.17 Rouse himself, in the final edition of The Tainos, acknowledged the limitations of his classificatory framework while defending its overall coherence.18

The record has gaps. Large parts of Hispaniola remain inadequately surveyed. Jamaica's pre-Columbian archaeology is thin by comparison with Puerto Rico's. Cuba's eastern provinces, where Taíno settlement was densest, have received less attention than they merit. Wooden objects, textiles, and basketry, which the Taíno produced in quantity, have almost entirely perished in the Caribbean climate. We are reconstructing a civilization from its stone, its ceramics, and its bones, filtered through the accounts of the people who destroyed it. The chapters that follow work within those constraints.
