Chapter Two

Society and Power

By the late fifteenth century, the Taíno of Hispaniola had organized themselves into a political system of considerable scale. Five regional polities, each called a cacicazgo, divided the island among them. Each was governed by a cacique whose authority was both political and ceremonial. The system was not a loose confederation of villages. It was a ranked society with hereditary leadership, a defined class structure, and institutional mechanisms for resolving disputes between polities. When Columbus landed on the northern coast in December 1492, he encountered not scattered bands of foragers but the administrative apparatus of a complex chiefdom.1

The Five Cacicazgos of Hispaniola

Spanish sources, particularly Bartolomé de las Casas and Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo, record five cacicazgos at contact, though their precise boundaries remain debated and almost certainly shifted over time. The fact that European chroniclers imposed their own categories of territorial sovereignty onto Taíno political organization complicates any reconstruction. Still, the broad outlines are recoverable.2

Maguá occupied the northeastern portion of the island. Its cacique, Guarionex, governed from the interior valley near modern La Vega, in the vicinity of the site later called Santo Cerro. The Cibao Valley, the most agriculturally productive region on the island, fell within Maguá's territory, and its fertile alluvial soils sustained a dense population. Guarionex would later become one of the principal figures in Taíno resistance to Spanish encomienda labor demands, a trajectory that ended with his capture and death in a shipwreck while being transported to Spain.3

Marién, in the northwest, was ruled by Guacanagaríx. He was the first cacique to establish sustained contact with Columbus, offering hospitality after the wreck of the Santa María on Christmas Eve, 1492. Spanish accounts portray Guacanagaríx as cooperative, even accommodating, though it is worth considering how much of this characterization reflects European expectations about compliant indigenous leaders rather than Guacanagaríx's actual political calculus. He may well have seen the newcomers as potential allies in inter-cacicazgo rivalries.4

Maguana, the interior cacicazgo centered on the central mountain ranges, was governed by Caonabó. The chroniclers describe him as the most aggressive of the five caciques in his dealings with the Spanish, and Las Casas records that Caonabó was originally from the Lucayan Bahamas or possibly of Carib descent. Whether this origin story is factual or represents Spanish narrative framing (casting the most resistant leader as an outsider) remains uncertain. Caonabó attacked the garrison Columbus left at La Navidad, and the Spanish eventually captured him through deception; he died in transit across the Atlantic.5

Jaragua, in the southwest, was the largest cacicazgo by territory. Its cacique at contact was Bohechío, whose sister Anacaona would succeed him around 1500. Jaragua had a reputation in Spanish sources for cultural sophistication: the chroniclers note the quality of its areíto performances, the skill of its artisans, and the political authority of its ruling lineage. Whether Jaragua was genuinely the cultural center of Hispaniola or whether the chroniclers were projecting their own ideas about courtly refinement is a question the sources cannot fully resolve. What is clear is that Anacaona's subsequent execution by the Spanish governor Nicolás de Ovando in 1503, carried out under the pretext of a diplomatic meeting, became one of the defining atrocities of the early colonial period.6

Higüey, in the southeast, was governed by a cacique known in the sources as Cayacoa or, later, Cotubanamá. This cacicazgo controlled the eastern tip of the island, including the passage to Puerto Rico. Cotubanamá led the last sustained military resistance to Spanish colonization on Hispaniola before his capture and hanging in Santo Domingo in 1504.7

The Cacique: Political and Spiritual Authority

The cacique was not merely a political administrator. The office carried spiritual weight. A cacique controlled access to the most powerful zemís (spirit objects), presided over major ceremonies, and mediated between the community and the supernatural forces that governed agriculture, weather, and warfare. Samuel Wilson has argued that the cacique's power was inseparable from his or her ritual role: political legitimacy rested on the ability to demonstrate spiritual efficacy, particularly in ensuring successful harvests and favorable outcomes in conflict.8

Caciques lived in larger bohíos (houses) than commoners, sometimes in structures described by the Spanish as caney (rectangular rather than circular). They received tribute in the form of labor and agricultural surplus. They wore distinctive regalia, including guanín (gold-copper alloy) pendants, elaborate cotton garments, and feathered headdresses. But the cacique's authority, while hereditary, was not absolute in the way European monarchy was. The chroniclers record instances of caciques consulting with nitaínos (nobles) before major decisions, and the social contract appears to have involved reciprocal obligations: the cacique provided redistribution of food, organized collective labor projects, and maintained diplomatic relationships with neighboring polities.9

Class Structure: Nitaínos and Naborías

Below the cacique, Taíno society was divided into at least two discernible classes. The nitaínos formed the noble stratum. They served as sub-chiefs, war leaders, advisors, and administrators of villages within the cacicazgo. The behique (shaman or healer) occupied a separate but related position of authority, functioning as the primary intermediary with the spirit world through cohoba ceremonies. Whether behiques constituted a distinct class or were drawn from the nitaíno ranks is not entirely clear from the sources.10

The naborías comprised the laboring majority. They cultivated conucos (mounded agricultural plots), fished, constructed canoes, built houses, and produced the material goods that sustained the cacicazgo. The Spanish later appropriated the term naboría to describe a form of indigenous servitude within the colonial labor system, which has complicated scholarly understanding of the pre-contact meaning. Whether naborías in Taíno society were a hereditary underclass, a working population with limited social mobility, or something more fluid than either of those categories remains debated. Las Casas described them as servants, but Las Casas was writing within a framework shaped by Iberian social categories, and his terminology may not map cleanly onto Taíno realities.11

Matrilineal Succession and the Role of Women

Taíno succession did not follow the patrilineal pattern familiar to European observers. Instead, the office of cacique passed matrilineally: from a man to his sister's son, not to his own biological son. This meant that a cacique's heir was the child of his sister, whose maternal lineage carried the legitimate claim. The system ensured that elite status was determined through the female line, and it gave women of the ruling families a structural importance that the Spanish chroniclers sometimes noted with surprise.12

Women could hold the office of cacique directly. The most prominent example is Anacaona, who governed Jaragua after the death of her brother Bohechío around 1500. Anacaona was not a regent or placeholder. Spanish sources describe her exercising full political authority: receiving diplomatic delegations, presiding over areítos, and commanding the allegiance of subordinate nitaínos. She was also renowned as a composer of areíto songs and poetry, a role that carried political as well as artistic significance in a society that transmitted law, history, and cosmological knowledge through oral performance.13

Beyond the cacica role, women occupied positions of considerable importance in daily life. They were the primary cultivators of cassava, the staple crop, and they controlled the complex, labor-intensive process of converting bitter manioc into edible bread (a procedure involving grating, pressing, and baking that required specialized knowledge and tools). Women produced cotton textiles, made pottery, and played a central role in household ritual. Lynne Guitar has emphasized that the matrilineal system was not merely a rule of succession but a reflection of a broader social logic in which women's productive and reproductive labor was recognized as foundational.14

The colonial encounter disrupted matrilineal succession almost immediately. Spanish administrators, unable or unwilling to recognize female authority or lateral inheritance, imposed patrilineal models that erased the structural role of women in Taíno governance. By the second decade of the sixteenth century, the cacicazgo system itself had been dismantled.

The Areíto: Ceremony as Archive

The areíto was the principal collective ceremony of Taíno society, and its functions extended well beyond entertainment. In a culture without writing, the areíto served as the primary vehicle for preserving and transmitting historical memory, genealogical knowledge, cosmological narratives, and social norms. Pané, Las Casas, and Oviedo all describe areítos at length, though their accounts vary in detail and reliability.15

An areíto combined dance, song, instrumental music, and recitation. Participants, sometimes numbering in the hundreds, moved in coordinated formations. A tekina (a guide or dance master, who could be male or female) led the performance, establishing the songs and choreography that the assembled participants followed in a call-and-response pattern. The tekina's role was part choreographer, part cantor, part historian. Getting the words and sequences right mattered: the areíto encoded information that the community depended on, from the genealogies of ruling families to the proper timing of agricultural cycles.

Areítos marked a range of occasions. Chroniclers describe performances celebrating solstices, successful harvests, marriages within the cacique's family, the arrival of visiting caciques from neighboring polities, and military victories. Some areítos lasted for days, fueled by chicha (fermented corn or cassava beer) and punctuated by ritual offerings. The ceremonial context set the areíto apart from secular performance: to participate was to affirm one's membership in the community and one's acceptance of the shared narratives that held it together.16

The mnemonic function deserves particular emphasis. Without written records, every piece of communal knowledge (legal precedent, territorial boundaries, origin stories, the obligations of kinship) had to be stored in human memory and transmitted through performance. The areíto was the institutional mechanism for this transmission. Its structured, repetitive format (call-and-response, repeated refrains, rhythmic patterning) was not accidental but functional: these are precisely the features that aid memorization across generations. When the colonial system suppressed the areíto, it did not merely end a cultural practice. It severed the primary means by which Taíno societies maintained their collective knowledge.17

The Ball Game: Batey

The Taíno ball game, called batey or batu, was played on rectangular courts that were themselves called batey. These courts were lined with stone slabs, many of which bore petroglyphs depicting zemí figures, celestial symbols, and anthropomorphic designs. The game used a rubber ball (made from the latex of the Hevea tree or possibly from local Castilla elastica), which players struck with their hips, elbows, shoulders, and heads. Hands and feet were not permitted.18

Teams typically consisted of ten to thirty players, and matches could involve men, women, or mixed groups. The game had clear sporting dimensions: the chroniclers describe spectators, competitive intensity, and physical skill. But it also served political and judicial functions. Inter-cacicazgo disputes could be settled through batey matches rather than warfare. Wagering accompanied the games, with caciques staking agricultural goods, cotton, guanín objects, and (according to some accounts) the labor of naborías. José Oliver has argued that the batey court functioned as a political arena in the most literal sense, a bounded space where competing claims were adjudicated through ritualized physical contest rather than open violence.19

The best-preserved ball court complex in the Caribbean is at Caguana, in the mountainous interior of Puerto Rico. The site contains ten stone-lined courts of varying sizes, bordered by monolithic slabs carved with elaborate petroglyphic designs. The largest court at Caguana measures roughly 50 by 30 meters, large enough to accommodate significant numbers of players and spectators. The petroglyphs on the bordering stones include depictions of faces, possibly ancestral or zemí figures, that would have observed the game from the perimeter, reinforcing the sacred dimension of the contest. Archaeological evidence suggests the site was in active use from approximately AD 1200 to 1500, spanning the period during which Taíno chiefdoms consolidated their political authority across the Greater Antilles.20

The ball game was not unique to the Taíno. Mesoamerican civilizations had their own rubber ball games with deep ritual significance, and scholars have debated the degree of connection (direct transmission, independent invention, or a shared ancestral practice) between the Caribbean and Mesoamerican traditions. Irving Rouse noted structural similarities while cautioning against assuming direct diffusion, and the question remains open.21

The Limits of the Record

Everything written above rests on a filtered archive. The Spanish chroniclers observed Taíno society during the years of its destruction. They wrote in service of colonial administration, religious conversion, or (in Las Casas's case) moral advocacy. None of them spoke Taíno fluently. Their descriptions of social structure often map European categories onto indigenous realities, and the absence of Taíno-authored texts means there is no corrective voice from within the society itself. Archaeology helps, but material culture cannot answer every question about social organization, gender roles, or the content of areíto performances.

The scholarship of Wilson, Guitar, Oliver, and Rouse has done much to reconstruct Taíno political and social life from these imperfect sources. Their work proceeds with appropriate caution, distinguishing between what the evidence supports and what it merely suggests. This chapter follows that example. The cacicazgo system, the matrilineal succession pattern, the areíto, and the batey are all attested in multiple independent sources and confirmed (to varying degrees) by archaeological evidence. The details within each of those categories, the precise powers of a cacique, the exact mechanics of class mobility, the full content of any given areíto performance, remain partially obscured. That obscurity is itself part of the record, a consequence of the colonial process that destroyed the society while simultaneously producing the only written accounts of it.22
