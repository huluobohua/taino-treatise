Chapter Seven

Legacy and Reclamation

By the middle of the sixteenth century, the Taíno had been declared finished. Spanish colonial administrators, chroniclers, and priests wrote of the indigenous population of the Greater Antilles in the past tense, recording their customs as curiosities of a vanished race. That judgment hardened over the following centuries into a settled fact: the Taíno were extinct, destroyed by disease, enslavement, and the violence of conquest. The narrative was convenient. If the indigenous people of the Caribbean no longer existed, then indigenous claims to land, political authority, and cultural recognition were moot. Colonial and later national governments could build their legitimacy on ground they declared empty of prior claimants. The extinction story was not merely a historical observation; it was a political instrument.

But the story was wrong, or at best radically incomplete. Genetic research, linguistic survival, persistent agricultural practices, community self-identification, and the testimony of people who never stopped knowing what they were have, since the late twentieth century, forced a revision. The Taíno were not annihilated. They were absorbed, marginalized, officially denied, and then, in many cases, quietly persistent. The question today is not whether Taíno heritage survives in the Caribbean. The evidence that it does is overwhelming. The harder questions concern what that survival means: who can claim it, on what terms, and with what political and cultural consequences.

The Extinction Myth and Its Uses

The declaration of Taíno extinction served different masters at different moments. For the sixteenth-century Spanish administration, acknowledging indigenous survival would have complicated the legal framework of the encomienda and the emerging plantation economy. For nineteenth-century creole nationalists in Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Dominican Republic, the Taíno became available as romantic ancestors precisely because they were understood to be safely dead: they could appear on coats of arms and in nationalist poetry without presenting any living constituency that might demand land, representation, or sovereignty. In the twentieth century, Caribbean anthropology and history largely accepted the extinction consensus, and scholars who studied the pre-Columbian period treated it as exactly that, a period with a definitive end.1

The persistence of this narrative owed something to the categories of race that governed Caribbean social thought. Under Spanish colonial racial taxonomy, and under the systems that followed it, indigenous identity was understood as a discrete biological category that could be diluted out of existence through intermarriage. When Taíno individuals married Africans or Spaniards (or, more commonly, were coerced into sexual relationships with them), their descendants were reclassified. Within a few generations, people who carried indigenous ancestry, indigenous knowledge, and indigenous cultural practices were officially categorized as mestizo, mulato, or simply campesino. The administrative erasure preceded and enabled the historical one.2

Genetic Evidence: What DNA Tells and What It Does Not

The first systematic challenge to the extinction narrative came from molecular genetics. In 2001, the geneticist Juan Carlos Martínez-Cruzado of the University of Puerto Rico at Mayagüez published results from a mitochondrial DNA study of 800 Puerto Ricans, sampled to represent the island's population proportionally. The findings were striking: 61.1 percent of all individuals in the study carried Amerindian mitochondrial DNA, compared with 26.4 percent carrying African mitochondrial lineages and 12.5 percent carrying Caucasian ones. Among participants who reported maternal Native American ancestry, the figure rose to 69.6 percent. The Amerindian mitochondrial haplogroups A and C accounted for 56.0 percent and 35.6 percent of the indigenous lineages, respectively.3

Because mitochondrial DNA is inherited exclusively through the maternal line, these results traced a specific history: Taíno women survived the conquest in sufficient numbers that their maternal lineage became the majority mitochondrial ancestry of the modern Puerto Rican population. This was consistent with the documented colonial pattern in which Spanish men (the colonial migration was overwhelmingly male) formed unions with indigenous and African women. The Y-chromosome picture was predictably different, dominated by European lineages, reflecting the gendered asymmetry of colonial reproduction.4

In 2018, a team led by Hannes Schroeder published the first ancient genome from the precolonial Caribbean: DNA extracted from the tooth of a Lucayan Taíno woman who had lived on Eleuthera, in the Bahamas, approximately one thousand years ago (eighth to tenth century). The genomic analysis, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, showed that her closest living genetic relatives were present-day Arawakan-speaking populations in northern South America, particularly in Venezuela and Colombia. The study also compared her genome to those of contemporary Caribbean people and found that present-day Puerto Ricans carry genetic signatures consistent with indigenous Caribbean ancestry. Biological continuity between the precolonial Taíno and living Caribbean populations was demonstrable at the genomic level.5

These results were significant, but they require careful interpretation. Genetic ancestry and cultural identity are not the same thing. Carrying Taíno mitochondrial DNA does not make a person Taíno in any politically or culturally meaningful sense, any more than carrying Norse genetic markers makes an English person a Viking. Indigenous identity, in the frameworks that indigenous peoples themselves have articulated, involves community membership, cultural practice, recognized lineage, and (in most cases) acceptance by an existing community. DNA can confirm biological descent; it cannot, by itself, constitute a people. The genetic data demolished the extinction claim as a biological proposition, but the cultural and political questions required different kinds of evidence.6

Cultural Survivals

Alongside the genetic record stands a body of cultural evidence that never really required scientific confirmation, because it was always in plain view. The Spanish language as spoken across the Caribbean is saturated with Taíno vocabulary. The count runs to over three thousand words in Caribbean Spanish: canoa (canoe), hamaca (hammock), huracán (hurricane), tabaco (tobacco), barbacoa (barbecue), batata (sweet potato), maíz (corn), along with terms for landscape, flora, fauna, and material culture that remained in use because the things they named remained in use.7 Several of these words entered global languages through Spanish and Portuguese: canoe, hammock, hurricane, tobacco, barbecue, potato, maize. Every English speaker who uses the word "hurricane" is speaking Taíno.

The place names of the Caribbean are a map of the Taíno world. Cuba retains its Taíno name, as do Haiti (Ayiti, "mountainous land"), Jamaica (Xaymaca, "land of wood and water"), and the indigenous name for Puerto Rico, Borikén. Across Hispaniola, major regions carry Taíno toponyms: Quisqueya, Cibao, Higüey, Samaná, Bayaguana. These names survived because the Spanish adopted them as practical labels for places they were governing, but their persistence also reflects a deeper continuity. The landscape was still being described, and often worked, on terms the Taíno had established.

Agricultural practices tell a similar story. Conuco farming, the Taíno system of mounded cultivation that predates European contact by centuries, persists in rural Hispaniola to this day. Farmers in the Dominican countryside and in Haiti's interior work conucos using methods recognizably descended from Taíno practice, growing cassava, sweet potatoes, and other crops on raised mounds in a system adapted to tropical soils and rainfall. The knowledge was transmitted not through academic reconstruction but through continuous practice, passed from parent to child across the colonial period and beyond. Bohío, the Taíno word for a thatched dwelling, remained the standard term for rural housing in the Dominican Republic well into the twentieth century, and the architectural form it described persisted alongside the word.8

Revivalist Movements

The late twentieth century saw the emergence of organized movements explicitly claiming Taíno identity. These movements were shaped by the broader context of indigenous rights activism in the Americas, the approach of the 1992 Columbus Quincentenary (which galvanized indigenous political mobilization across the hemisphere), and the accumulating genetic and cultural evidence that made the extinction narrative untenable.

The Jatibonicu Taíno Tribal Nation of Borikén, based in Puerto Rico's central mountains and in New Jersey, traces its organizational history to the 1968 Taíno Indian Movement of Puerto Rico. Under the leadership of Pedro Guanikeyu Torres, the Jatibonicu Taíno have campaigned for official recognition as an indigenous people, maintaining that their community represents an unbroken line of descent from the pre-Columbian population of Puerto Rico's mountainous interior, where colonial control was always weakest and indigenous communities persisted longest.9

The United Confederation of Taíno People (UCTP), founded in 1998 and based in New York and Puerto Rico, operates not as a tribe but as a representative institution bringing together various Taíno communities and organizations. The UCTP has engaged international bodies, campaigning at the United Nations Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues and through the International Indian Treaty Council for the recognition of Taíno peoples within the frameworks established by the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007). Roberto Múkaro Borrero, the UCTP's president, has been a consistent advocate for Taíno representation in international indigenous rights forums.10

Jorge Baracutay Estevez, a Hiwayawa Taíno cultural activist and scholar, has been among the most visible figures in the reclamation movement since the late 1980s. Estevez founded the Higuayagua Taíno of the Caribbean and has worked extensively with the Smithsonian Institution's National Museum of the American Indian (NMAI). His work has combined genealogical research, cultural preservation, and public education, making the case for Taíno survival to audiences that had long accepted the extinction narrative without question. Estevez's involvement in the 1992 Quincentenary counter-commemorations helped establish Taíno reclamation as a visible political and cultural movement rather than a marginal curiosity.11

The Smithsonian NMAI exhibition "Taíno: Native Heritage and Identity in the Caribbean," curated by Ranald Woodaman with José Barreiro and Jorge Estevez, brought the question of Taíno survival to a wide public audience. The exhibition and the associated Caribbean Indigenous Legacies Project (CILP) presented archaeological, genetic, and ethnographic evidence alongside the voices of contemporary Taíno communities, treating the question of Taíno persistence as a matter of documented fact rather than romantic aspiration.12

Dominican Identity Politics and the Taíno

The Dominican Republic presents a particularly complex case. The Dominican state has historically promoted a narrative of national identity grounded in Spanish-indigenous mestizaje: the Dominican people as descendants of Spaniards and Taíno, with African ancestry minimized or denied. This framework of indigenismo performs several political functions simultaneously. It provides a pre-Columbian pedigree for Dominican national identity. It differentiates Dominicans from Haitians (whose national identity is grounded in African descent and the slave revolution), reinforcing the anti-Haitian racism that has structured Dominican politics since the nineteenth century. And it does all this through a figure, the Taíno ancestor, understood to be safely historical, making no contemporary claims.13

Taíno symbols are woven into the Dominican Republic's official iconography. The national coat of arms, the names of provinces and cities, the imagery of tourist marketing: all invoke Taíno heritage as a source of national distinction. The word "quisqueyano," derived from the Taíno name for Hispaniola, appears in the national anthem. But this official celebration of Taíno identity has historically been disconnected from any serious engagement with indigenous history, archaeology, or contemporary community claims. The Taíno ancestor, in this usage, is a symbol emptied of political content, invoked to anchor Dominican identity while the actual history of indigenous survival, resistance, and cultural transmission goes unexamined.14

The tension is real and should not be flattened into a simple accusation. Many Dominicans who identify with Taíno heritage do so sincerely, drawing on family traditions, local knowledge, and a genuine sense of connection to the pre-Columbian past. The problem lies not in the identification itself but in the political framework that has instrumentalized it: celebrating indigenous ancestry as a way of denying African ancestry, using the Taíno past to make claims about Dominican exceptionalism while doing nothing to support the scholarly or community work that might give that past substance. Lynne Guitar, a historian who has written extensively on Dominican identity and Taíno survival, has documented both the sincerity of local indigenous identification in the Dominican countryside and the ways it has been manipulated by national elites.15

Cuba, the Diaspora, and Other Contexts

Cuba's relationship to Taíno heritage follows a different trajectory. The island's eastern provinces, particularly the region around Baracoa, were among the last areas of the Caribbean where indigenous communities maintained a degree of autonomy. Cuban scholars and cultural organizations have documented Taíno heritage sites, and communities in Oriente province have maintained traditions (including specific agricultural and artisanal practices) that they identify as indigenous in origin. The Cuban revolutionary government's approach to indigenous heritage has been shaped by its broader ideological commitments: indigenous history is acknowledged as part of Cuba's national patrimony, but within a framework that emphasizes class rather than ethnic identity.16

The diaspora has been central to the Taíno reclamation movement. New York, home to the largest Puerto Rican and Dominican populations outside the Caribbean, became a primary site for Taíno cultural organizing from the 1970s onward. The UCTP is headquartered there. Estevez has done much of his work from New York. The diaspora context shaped the movement in specific ways: distance from the Caribbean islands created both the desire to recover ancestral identity and the organizational infrastructure (access to museums, universities, media, and international institutions) that made organized advocacy possible. The 1992 Quincentenary, experienced in New York through counter-demonstrations and cultural events, was a catalytic moment for diaspora Taíno organizing.17

The Politics of Identity Claims

Not everyone accepts Taíno revivalist claims at face value, and the skepticism is not merely reactionary. Gabriel Haslip-Viera, a historian at the City College of New York, has written critically about what he terms the "Taíno revival," arguing that some identity claims rest on selective genealogy, romanticized history, and assertions of cultural continuity that the evidence does not support. Haslip-Viera does not deny that indigenous ancestry is widespread in the Caribbean; the genetic data make that undeniable. His concern is with the leap from genetic ancestry to cultural identity, and with the political dynamics of a revival movement that, in his view, sometimes overstates the coherence and continuity of its traditions.18

These critiques have provoked sharp responses from Taíno activists and their academic allies, who point out that Haslip-Viera's framework implicitly accepts the colonial standard under which indigenous identity can be destroyed by administrative fiat and cultural disruption. If the bar for legitimate indigenous identity is unbroken cultural transmission of a kind that colonialism made impossible, then the standard itself perpetuates the colonial erasure it claims merely to observe. The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007) affirms the right of indigenous peoples to self-identification, but its application to Caribbean communities whose cultural continuity was violently disrupted raises questions that the Declaration's framers did not fully anticipate.19

The debate touches a genuine difficulty. Indigenous identity, as understood by most indigenous peoples and by the international frameworks that have emerged to protect indigenous rights, is not purely genetic. It involves community: a group of people who recognize one another as belonging together. It involves cultural practice: knowledge, language, ceremony, subsistence patterns, and ways of relating to the land. It involves recognized lineage: descent acknowledged within the community, not merely demonstrated by a laboratory. And it involves, in most frameworks, acceptance by the broader indigenous world. Some Taíno revivalist groups meet these criteria more convincingly than others. Some have been received by other indigenous peoples; others have been regarded with suspicion. The variation is real and should not be obscured by either blanket endorsement or blanket dismissal.

What can be said with confidence is this: the biological extinction of the Taíno is a myth. The cultural extinction, while closer to the truth, overstates the completeness of the erasure. Thousands of Taíno words survive in daily use. Agricultural systems rooted in Taíno practice persist. Place names hold the memory of a Taíno geography. Genetic studies have confirmed what community memory never forgot. The question is no longer whether the Taíno survived but what form that survival takes, who speaks for it, and what rights and recognitions follow from it. These are political questions as much as historical ones, and they remain open.

What Survives and What Is Made

A final distinction is worth drawing carefully. There is a difference between cultural survival and cultural reconstruction, and both are legitimate, but they are not the same thing. When a Dominican farmer works a conuco using methods transmitted without interruption from the pre-Columbian period, that is survival. When a diaspora community in New York reconstructs a ceremony based on archaeological and ethnohistorical evidence, consulting with other Arawakan peoples in South America to fill gaps in the Caribbean record, that is reconstruction. Both represent ways of engaging with Taíno heritage, but they carry different kinds of authority and face different kinds of challenge.20

Reconstruction is not fraud. Many indigenous peoples around the world have rebuilt cultural practices that colonial violence interrupted. The Māori language revival in New Zealand, the resurgence of potlatch ceremonies on the Northwest Coast, the reconstruction of Hawaiian navigation traditions: all involved deliberate, self-conscious recovery of knowledge that had been suppressed or nearly lost. The Taíno case is more extreme because the disruption was earlier and more complete, but the principle is the same. A people's right to reclaim their heritage is not contingent on that heritage having survived intact.

The scholars who have contributed most to understanding Taíno legacy, among them Martínez-Cruzado, Schroeder, Estevez, Guitar, and Barreiro, have generally treated the question with the care it demands: acknowledging what the evidence supports, noting where it is ambiguous, and refusing to let either the extinction myth or its opposite (an unbroken, uncontested Taíno continuity) stand unchallenged. The truth, as is usually the case with colonial history, is harder than either version. A civilization was nearly destroyed. It was not entirely destroyed. What remains is partial, contested, and precious, and the work of understanding it continues.
