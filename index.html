The Taíno World

People, cosmology, and material life in the precolonial Caribbean, and the long afterlife of a civilization declared extinct

This treatise examines the Taíno peoples of the Caribbean: the civilization they built across Hispaniola, Puerto Rico, Jamaica, Cuba, and the Bahamas before 1492, the colonial encounter that shattered it, and the ongoing reclamation of Taíno identity by communities who refused the narrative of disappearance.

The Taíno left no written language. What we know comes from archaeology, from the filtered accounts of Spanish chroniclers (Fray Ramón Pané, Bartolomé de las Casas, Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo), from linguistic survival in Caribbean Spanish, and from the material record: stone zemís buried in planting mounds, ball courts carved into mountain valleys, ceramic vessels depicting mythological scenes that Pané's Relación allows us to partially decode. The scholarly work of Irving Rouse, William Keegan, José Oliver, Corinne Hofman, and others has reconstructed much of the Taíno world, but significant gaps remain. This treatise acknowledges those gaps rather than smoothing over them.

A thread on numerical and structural symbolism runs through the work, particularly the significance of three and four in Taíno cosmology and material culture. This is not mystical numerology. It is a way of reading how the Taíno encoded their understanding of the cosmos in the objects they made, the stories they told, and the spaces they built.

The treatise does not attempt to be comprehensive. It cannot address every island community, every regional variation, every ongoing scholarly debate. It focuses on the Greater Antilles, with particular attention to Hispaniola and Puerto Rico, because those are the islands where the archaeological record is deepest, the ethnohistorical sources richest, and the contemporary identity politics most charged.
